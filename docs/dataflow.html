How the data moves, and what carries it

A frame leaves a UE4 render target and, about 130 milliseconds later, becomes a velocity setpoint. It crosses four protocols and two Python interpreters to get there. This is the route, the reason for each hop, and the places the route bites back.

01 The seam that shapes everything

The single most consequential fact about this system is that it cannot be one process. The CARLA-Air client is an ABI-tagged libcarla.cpython-310 extension; ROS 2 Jazzy ships Python 3.12. Neither interpreter can load the other's C extensions — this was measured in both directions, not assumed.

Why a socket rather than shared memory

Three ways out existed: install ROS 2 Humble alongside (also 3.10, but a second ROS distro and nodes that could not be shared with the sibling project), push frames through shared memory, or accept one serialisation hop. The hop costs about 1 ms against a capture that costs 500, so it was never the bottleneck worth optimising.

sim_bridge/protocol.py is imported by both interpreters — the same file, not a copy. A wire format that drifts between the two halves of a bridge is a classic way to lose a day.

02 Four sockets into AirSim, not one

Image capture takes about 250 ms; a telemetry read takes 0.2 ms. On one connection every odometry read queued behind the current capture, and odometry arrived at 1.5 Hz. The fix took three rounds, each addressing a different cause.

The trap this design keeps re-teaching

The locks guard dispatch classes, not sockets. That mismatch has produced three separate failures: a follow thread racing reset on one socket under two different locks (surfacing as Existing exports of data: object cannot be re-sized from inside tornado, which names nothing useful); a self-deadlock when a method took a lock the dispatcher already held; and land blocking for a whole descent while both video streams sat behind it on the same lock.

MEDIA exists because of the third. Video now cannot queue behind anything.

03 One decision, end to end

See-Point-Fly is a slow generator over a fast controller. Every node boundary exists to keep those two decoupled — the model runs near 1 Hz while setpoints stream at 10.

Two rules inside that loop

Frames arriving during inference are dropped, not queued. A model answering a four-second-old frame is worse than one answering the current frame late.

The controller never stops sending. A gap with no setpoint is exactly when an aircraft runs away, so it streams a zero-velocity hold rather than nothing.

Why the topics are PX4-shaped when there is no PX4

The simulator contains no MAVLink, no uORB and no PX4 — the flight controller is AirSim's SimpleFlight. Publishing /fmu/out/* anyway is a deliberate shim, and it buys one thing: the autonomy nodes are written against the same interface a real Pixhawk 6C exposes over uXRCE-DDS. Moving to hardware means deleting one node, not rewriting the stack. QoS matches PX4's — best-effort, transient-local, depth 5 — so a node that works here does not fail on hardware over a QoS mismatch.

What it does not give you: timestamps are host time, not a PX4 boot clock; arming_state follows an API flag with no failsafe state machine behind it; acceleration and jerk in the setpoint are ignored because SimpleFlight has nowhere to put them.

Why polling five sensors costs an image

The sensors themselves are nearly free; the round trips are not. The sidecar is one Python process, and msgpack-rpc holds the GIL while it serialises — so a hundred extra calls per second slow the media thread even though it is on a different socket, under a different lock. The ground-truth environment read is cached for 5 s because air temperature and density do not move over a flight; that was 20% of the call for no new information.

The LiDAR sees less than its configuration claims

points_per_second counts rays cast, not points returned. What actually binds is range against lower_fov: with a level sensor and lower_fov: -50, the steepest available ray is 50° below horizontal, so the slant range to the ground is AGL / sin(50°). Against an 80 m range that puts the ground out of reach above about 61 m AGL.

Measured over the plaza: 390 points at 107 m AGL, 735 at 67 m, 1584 at 47 m — and 443 at 67 m over open water, where there are no buildings to intercept anything. avoid_the_block flies at 120 m AGL, where this sensor returns the tower and no ground at all.

The two cameras are not the same camera

The drone camera is a measurement surface: 640×480, its resolution sets the model's token cost, its 4:3 aspect must match the depth buffer or grounding samples the wrong pixel, and its field of view decides where level flight lands in the frame. Changing it changes the experiment.

The chase camera is a CARLA sensor, scored on nothing, and free to be pretty. It renders inside the same UE4 process and pushes frames asynchronously, so it never queues behind an AirSim capture. At 30 Hz it costs the simulator nothing measurable — 59.8 against 60.0 ticks per second.

Real-time factor will lie to you

Push the chase camera to 60 Hz at 1080p and the simulator's tick rate drops from 60 to 49.5 — while RTF stays at 1.000. CARLA stretches delta_seconds so simulated time keeps tracking wall time, so a slowed simulator looks perfectly healthy on the metric you would naturally check, and is quietly integrating physics on a coarser step (16.7 ms → 20.3 ms). Judge the simulator by tick rate, not RTF.
